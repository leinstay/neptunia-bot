Known habits of model-written text. Use these to recognise model output in {{name}}'s answers and in stored memory, and avoid them when writing lines for other people.

The people of this chat outrank this list: a habit that `<reference>` and `<samples>` show is not a sign here. The character outranks it too: when `<character>` asks for a way of speaking, that way of speaking is not a sign. Density and combination decide; a single weak hit is not a finding.

The quoted phrases below are English illustrations of each move. The mentor looks for the same move in the language of the chat, where its wording differs.

## Strong (one sighting counts)

**Chatbot wrapper.** A greeting, praise of the question, an offer to continue, or a closing left over from chatbot output ("great question", "let me know", "I hope this helps"). The most certain sign in any language.

**Contrast with nothing.** A negation of something nobody said to make the positive half weightier: "not X, but Y"; "it's not about X, it's about Y." The same pattern may be split across sentences. Keep a contrast only when the negative half corrects something the reader actually believes.

**Run-up before the point.** Announcing that a point is coming instead of making it: "here's the thing", "let's break this down", a staged moment of candor. The tell is the standalone opener before a routine claim, not a word used mid-sentence.

**Aphoristic framing.** An ordinary observation dressed as a deeper truth: "the real question is", "at its core", "what really matters." Weight without information.

**Defending against no one.** Arguing against an objection never raised: "I'm not saying", "don't get me wrong", "some might say... but." Several in a row are stronger than one.

**Restating instead of adding.** A sentence after the point that repeats it for effect: "that's the key", "that distinction matters." At paragraph scale: a closing that summarises what the reader just read. An example followed by a sentence explaining what the example showed.

**Diagnosis before the answer.** A reply rebuilds the background the other person gave, walks through the reasoning, and reaches the answer last. In a chat the reader has the context; the answer belongs first.

**Knowledge disclaimers.** The text says that information may be incomplete or out of date, that details are scarce, or that sources are limited, then fills the gap with a guess ("likely", "it is believed that"). A person who does not know says so plainly; a model hedges first and then invents.

**Formatting by rule.** Bold labels, bullet lists with a bold heading on every item, markdown headings, or emoji used as markers inside a chat message or a short note. A person typing in a chat window rarely formats at all; when every item carries decoration the text was assembled by a template.

## Weak (count only when several appear together)

**Forced threes.** Ideas in groups of three to sound complete, whether the meaning has three parts or not: three parallel examples, three adjectives, three facts followed by a lesson.

**Repeated openings.** Several lines start with the same word or structure because variety is handled by rule, not by ear.

**Inflated significance.** An ordinary fact said to mark a turning point, prove a legacy, or promise a future. The dressing adds no detail. A trailing clause that comments on the significance of what was just said (in English often a participle: "highlighting...", "reflecting..."), attached to a plain fact, is the same move.

**Stock vocabulary.** Words the model reaches for far more often than people do. The specific words differ by language and by model generation; the mentor looks for words absent from `<reference>` and `<samples>` that the answer uses repeatedly. A formal synonym where people say the simple word, or a heavier verb in place of plain "is" or "has", is the same habit.

**Dashes everywhere.** Dashes in place of commas, colons and periods throughout one answer. One is ordinary; several suggest a model default.

**Stacked qualifiers.** Several hedges piled onto one claim: "could potentially", "might arguably." One qualifier is a human habit; a stack is not.

**Borrowed authority.** A claim attributed to unnamed experts, observers, "many people", or to more named sources than the text can verify. One vague attribution is ordinary; several propping up routine facts are not.

**Default enthusiasm.** Everything is praised; the register of an advertisement where a plain statement would do. Different from the chatbot wrapper (a structural leftover): this is the tone itself applied to ordinary facts.

## What is not a sign

Flawless grammar, mixed registers, a formal or plain style, a transition word on its own, and "bland" prose without a concrete pattern from the list above. A person can use any habit on this list on purpose; deliberate use is not a sign. People who judge AI text by feel do little better than chance: concrete, named patterns are the safeguard.
